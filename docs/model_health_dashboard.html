What the middle square costs, and how far that number reaches

One question, asked once, on 300 frames fixed in advance. Scoring the middle square instead of a botanist’s outlines costs 6.3 points. This is where that comes from, and how far it reaches.

Which frames. 300 frames from 12 sites and 38 flight days, drawn from a fixed list before any of these numbers existed. Both ways of asking ran on every one of them, so the two rates come from the same frames.

What was compared. One Pl@ntNet call per crown a botanist had outlined, the answers pooled into one name for the frame by how much of it each crown covered. Against the fixed 1280×1280 square from the middle of a frame, 13.7% of the frame’s area, which is what every rate on this page uses.

How sure. We are 95% sure the true gap is between +3.9 and +8.9 points. Frames shot at the same site look alike, so the count was re-run 10,000 times, each time redrawing the 12 sites with replacement. We kept the middle 95% of the answers.

On 25 frames outlining got the name right where the middle square got it wrong. On 6 it went the other way.

How far the gap reaches.

  • One export batch. Every frame here carries the earlier file naming zoom. A later batch of flights was exported under the other naming, and no mission in this design draws from both.
  • 12 of the 17 field sites. Frames from a site are alike, so five unvisited sites are five unknowns.
  • Per frame, not per species. The sample carries 72 species, and the two commonest are 26.3% of its 300 frames. So the gap leans towards what the model already knows best. The plan asked for no per-species average here, so none is published.

The rules behind this gap predate the data: which frames, which test, what counts as right, and when we were allowed to look. The full read, both averagings, and the warnings the design requires verbatim sit with that design, and we send it on request.

Every rate here is scored on the fixed centre square, not on outlined crowns. So a low rate can mean the crop missed the tree rather than that the model missed the name. Read a row as a flag for a second look, not as that species’ identification accuracy.

  • Too few labels to judge Fewer than 10 labelled frames, so the score is too thin to trust yet.
  • Wrong even with enough labels Enough frames, but the first guess is still weak, so more labels will not fix it.
  • Mixed Mixed results, so keep it in the normal review queue.
  • Usually right Usually right, so this species is low priority for extra work.
  • Never returned on any BCI photo Pl@ntNet never returned this name on any BCI photo, not just on this species’ own frames. That is what we saw, not what the model can do. We ask each photo for 5 names. A species it carries but never ranks that high looks the same to us as one it does not carry at all. Other rows do show 0.0% in the “Top-5 accuracy” column under a different status. There the model did produce the name, just never on the frames of that species.
  • Not in the project's own species list Pl@ntNet's own species list, read by predict/fetch_checklist.py, does not carry this name. That is a proven absence, not the sample effect behind “never returned”.

Each species gets one status: the first rule that fits. Order: “not in the project's own species list”, “usually right”, “right name in the list, not first”. Then “never returned on any BCI photo”, “too few labels to judge”. A few-frame species can still show as “right name in the list, not first”. That is the point: it is cheap work whatever its count. Read the labelled-frames column next to the status.

107 of these 186 species start hidden. They carry fewer than 10 labelled frames each, the same cut-off as the “too few labels to judge” status. On that few frames a rate lands on a handful of values: F1 on one labelled frame can only be 0% or 100%. Species the model never returned stay on screen however few frames they carry. Type a name or pick a status to reach a hidden row, or tick show all 186.

Top-1 accuracy on a species row is that species’ recall: of the frames a botanist labelled it, the share the first guess got right. Precision asks the reverse, over the frames the model guessed that name on, and is only countable where a botanist has labelled the frame. So it is precision over the frames we scored, not over the survey. F1 is their harmonic mean, so a row scores well only when both do.

Model’s confidence is Pl@ntNet’s own score for its first guess, averaged over that species’ frames. Pl@ntNet spreads 100% of it across every species it knows. So 0.86 means nearly all of that went on one name, and 0.32 means it was spread thin. Middle half is where the middle 50% of that species’ frames fall, the 25th to the 75th percentile. A mean of 0.60 over a middle half of 0.55 to 0.65 is a steady score. The same mean over 0.20 to 0.95 is two behaviours averaged into one number, and the column sorts on that width.

59 of 186 species carry 10 or more labelled frames. 15 of those 59 are at or above top-1 accuracy. The other 127 species carry fewer frames than that. On that few a rate lands on a handful of values, so they are left out of this count.

Why the per-frame figure is one number and not three. Every frame here carries one botanist label and one first guess. A wrong guess is one miss for the labelled species and one false alarm for the guessed one. Both denominators are then the frame count, and precision, recall, F1 and top-1 accuracy work out identical.

Precision is measured on the frames we scored. A false alarm is only visible where a botanist has labelled the frame. So these are rates over the frames we scored, not over the survey. A species with no labelled frame has no row here at all: the per-species averages run over the 186 labelled species only.

A species the model never guesses scores 0% precision, not a blank. It has no right guesses to divide, and reading that as perfect precision on an empty list would flatter the average. Rows on very few frames are noisy the same way. Under 10 labelled frames a row starts hidden in the species table, and its F1 can only land on a couple of values.

The 51 single-frame species are 27% of the per-species bar and 2% of the per-frame one. Pl@ntNet is right 25.5% of the time on them, against 84.8% at 25 or more frames.

Misses differ at each end. On species with 4 frames or fewer, the right name is still in the 5 for 21.1% of 114 misses. At 25+ it is 50.5% of 368. Misses on common species are near misses; on rare ones the model does not know the plant.

Set aside species under 10 frames and the scores become 83.7% per frame and 77.5% per species. That is 6 points apart, not the 29 between 79.5% and 50.3%. A one-frame species scores only 0% or 100%, so those 51 votes are coin flips.

Read those rows as how common a species is, not as something labelling changed. These predictions come from a frozen Pl@ntNet regional model that has never seen a BCI label; common species simply have more reference photos inside Pl@ntNet already. Extra labels buy knowledge instead: under about 10 frames a per-species accuracy jumps around too much to act on.

Each group, with its species count, frame count and rates, is in support_buckets.csv.

Is the confidence score worth anything: mostly yes

Read this before treating a confidence as a probability. How often the first guess is right, band by band, over every labelled frame.

All 3,277 labelled frames, one guess each. The bands rise, so a higher score really does mean a likelier answer, which is what makes ordering the label queue on confidence work at all.

It is not a probability. A band reading 97.9% is not the same claim as a score of 0.9. And this holds in bulk only: on species with few labelled frames a high score is much less reliable, which the label-queue page measures band by band.

Every band, with the frames and the right guesses behind it, is in confidence_calibration.csv.

What the words mean: frame, label, crown, centre crop

Four words do all the work on this page. Which part of a photo was scored, and against which label, is the whole of the difference between the two numbers above.

  • A frame is one 4000×3000 drone photo.
  • A crown is one tree canopy a botanist outlined inside a frame.
  • A frame's label is the species whose outlined crowns cover the most area in the whole frame.
  • The centre crop is the fixed 1280×1280 square from the middle of a frame, 13.7% of the frame’s area. Every number below that covers all the labelled frames was scored on that square. We ask Pl@ntNet for 5 names per photo (nb-results=5). That is our request setting, not a limit of the model.
  • The first guess is the top-ranked of those names, and right means it matches the frame's label.
  • Outlining the trees first means something else. We ask Pl@ntNet about each crown on its own. Then we combine the answers into one name for the frame, weighted by how much of the frame each crown covers. That is the same rule the label itself is built from, which is why it is the fairer of the two numbers at the top.
Why three different frame counts: 3,277, 3,749 and 3,781

They do not contradict each other. Each counts a different thing.

3,277 frames carry a label naming a species. Every accuracy rate on this page is measured on those.

3,749 frames have a cached Pl@ntNet answer: the 3,277 above, plus 384 labelled only to a genus and 88 labelled only to a family.

3,781 frames a botanist has labelled at all, the 32 with no cached answer included.

Each number on this page says which of the three it is using.

How this was measured: the model, the frames, the files

Read this before quoting any number outside the team. It names the model, the request settings, and the one assumption that cannot be checked offline.

  • Predictions: model run unknown, the Central America regional model, so a regional restriction is already in place. That restriction excludes 3 species BCI has recorded, checked against the region’s own species list. All 3 of them are on the worldwide list, so the region, not the model, is why they never came back.
  • Request: nb-results=5, no-reject=true, organs detected automatically, include-related-images=false, on a 1280 px centre crop. A correct answer at position 6 or beyond was never returned and cannot be seen here.
  • Evaluated set: 3,277 frames across 186 species whose botanist label names a species rather than only a genus. They are the historical labelling record, not a random draw. These rates carry over to unlabelled frames only if unlabelled frames look like labelled ones, and that is not checkable offline.
  • Labels, in the merge script’s own words: “Ground truth merged from Labelbox export Export project - 2024_bci - 8_6_2026.ndjson on 2026-08-27.” The merge keeps the newer label, and that batch has had no Labelbox review step yet.
  • Snapshot: one dated model-health-<date>/ folder, the latest state, with no trend over earlier folders. Every number is recomputed from the source data at build time. It is checked against the 5 CSVs the measurement pass wrote there, and a mismatch aborts the build. To rebuild, see the README beside this dashboard’s source.
