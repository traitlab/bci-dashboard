The prioritised batches are in build/tables/send_batches.csv. Send from that file. This page shows the order and the reason behind each photo's place in it. The file holds 50 batches of at most 100 photos, each species group kept together. One batch there is one Labelbox batch, and global_key is the column Labelbox is given. To send batch 1: python labelling/dispatch_round.py --round 1 --csv build/tables/send_batches.csv --batch 1 --test sends the first five photos only, then the same command with no --test sends the rest. How Pl@ntNet scores against the labels is a separate page, model_health_dashboard.html.

This order is recomputed when the Pl@ntNet model tag changes, not on a calendar. Pl@ntNet ships a new model when its authors have one, roughly every two months. Until the tag above moves, re-running this page returns the same order, so work through the batches rather than waiting for a refresh.

This order has not been graded yet. Nothing here measures whether it fills gaps faster than sending photos at random. It is a reasonable guess about where our labels are thin. The wait rule further down is measured.

Sending a batch

send_batches.csv is this same queue packed into batches of 100, each species kept whole. Send batch 1 of round 1 with:

python3 labelling/dispatch_round.py --round 1 --csv build/tables/send_batches.csv --batch 1 --test

Drop --test once the dry run looks right.

44 frames are held out of this queue. They carry a split in splits.csv (6 test, 31 train, 7 valid), so they are part of how this page's own numbers are graded. Sending one back for labelling would put a new answer into the set those numbers are measured on. They are not lost: they are labelled work already accounted for elsewhere.

Some already have more than 10 labelled frames; they are here on the second half of the first row above, not the first.

6 unlabelled photos got no answer at all: the candidate list came back empty. Likeliest to be junk or to show no plant, and no automatic rule for junk is reliable, so check that handful by eye.

The later export batch is ungraded. Every frame scored here carries the earlier file naming zoom: all 3,277 of them. No botanist has labelled a frame carrying the later file naming tele, so this page says nothing about those. They are 445 of the queue (11.5%); sending them is how it becomes known. Both words read like camera settings and neither is one. The flight team confirmed the naming changed and nothing else did. Every tele frame comes from one of four flights that also produced zoom frames.

Why the queue is in this order

Ordering by look finds species faster than working down a random list. The charts below score that on photos already named, show where the ordering stops separating photos, and put the head of every queue on screen.

What this is measured on. The 1,719 photos a botanist has already named, carrying 155 species between them. Not the queue. It shows the method works where the answer is known.

Half those species take 129 photos in this order and 329 in a random one. Read it as evidence for the ordering, not as a promise about the queue: this run starts from nothing, and the queue starts from every photo already named.

How to read this. Each point averages a slice of the queue. The line falls, so photos near the top really are less like the named ones than photos near the bottom. Most of that fall is in the first quarter of the queue. Past it the line keeps dropping, but slowly. The photos it separates there differ from each other far less than the ones at the head do.

What this ordering costs. Of the 387 photos it puts first, 18.9% carry the newer file naming, against 11.5% across the whole queue. That naming marks a later batch of flights, not a different camera. Where the first share is the bigger one, the head leans on that batch. Some of what makes those photos look new is then the batch, not the species.

Which frames can wait, and why the line sits where it does

Read this to move the confidence line, or to check the wait rule. The rule in force is 0.8 or more sure, and the species has at least 10 labelled frames. It would let 199 of 479 frames held back for grading wait. It orders the queue, it does not close frames, and it is recomputed whenever Pl@ntNet updates.

Suggested rule: leave a frame for later when Pl@ntNet is at least 0.8 confident and its species already has 10 or more labelled frames. On the 479 frames held back for grading, that rule reaches 199 of them (41.5%), and the first guess is wrong on 1.5% of those.

What those 479 frames are. The labelled frames marked test in splits.csv. The rule was chosen on the other frames, so nothing here is graded on the frames that picked it. Every count below is out of those 479. They are not the model-health page's hold-out, a separate draw of 300 frames; the two overlap and neither contains the other.

Nothing here is a label. A frame that can wait keeps whatever label it has, or none; the rule only pushes it down the queue. The decision also expires with the model. Pl@ntNet ships a new one every few months, and re-running this page after that can bring any frame back to the top.

41 species reach 10 labelled frames inside the frames a rule may learn from, which is the second half of the rule. Counting every label gives a larger number, so this will not match the “too few labels to judge” count above.

Can we trust the confidence? In bulk yes, on rare species no

Over all frames at once the score is trustworthy: when the model is sure it is almost always right. That is what makes ordering the queue possible at all. On rarely-labelled species it is not, so ordering on confidence alone would push exactly the species you care about to the bottom:

Raising the confidence line does not repair this. Requiring the species to have been measured first does, which is why the suggested rule has two conditions.
