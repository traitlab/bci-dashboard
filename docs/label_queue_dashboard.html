For the labelling team: send a batch

send_batches.csv is this same queue packed into batches of 100, each species kept whole. To send a batch, after checking its status in Labelbox, use this command with the chosen round and batch numbers. This example selects batch 1 of round 1:

python3 labelling/dispatch_round.py --round 1 --csv build/tables/send_batches.csv --batch 1 --test

This creates a live five-photo batch and updates metadata in Labelbox. --test limits the send to five photos; it is not an offline preview. Omit it to send the full selected batch.

One batch there is one Labelbox batch, and global_key is the column Labelbox is given.

Two link columns, filled where we have one. The first opens the whole frame in any browser, no Labelbox seat and no login, and it is filled on 3,875 of 3,875 rows. The second opens the frame in the project it was labelled in, which is the only view that draws the crown being asked about, and it is filled on 3,430. The 445 rows without one are frames no Labelbox export names. Those cells are empty rather than guessed: a guessed link is a 404 in front of a botanist.

44 frames are kept out of this queue. They are held back for grading, so they are part of how this page's own numbers are graded. Sending one back for labelling would put a new answer into the set those numbers are measured on. They are not lost: they are labelled work already accounted for elsewhere.

6 unlabelled photos got no answer at all: the candidate list came back empty. Inspect these photos by eye; an empty answer does not establish that no plant is visible.

The later export batch is ungraded. No botanist has labelled a frame carrying the later file naming, tele, which is 445 of the queue frames (11.5%).

Both words read like camera settings and neither is one. The flight team confirmed the naming changed and nothing else did. Every tele frame comes from one of four flights that also produced zoom frames.

How to review model guesses

These statuses describe model performance, not biodiversity value. Use the photo queue to choose work; use these species-level results to decide how closely to check a guess.

  • 21 species Right name in the list, not first Compare the candidates with the photo; a lower-ranked guess may fit better
  • 32 species Too few labels to judge Inspect photos and add labelled evidence before trusting this species' rate
  • 2 species Often wrong on labelled frames Use manual identification or expert review; do not rely on the first guess
  • 17 species Mixed Check the guess against the photo; performance varies
  • 14 species Usually right Lower priority for checking model guesses. Keep labels needed for biodiversity work
  • 4 species Never returned on any BCI photo Use manual identification; absence from returned guesses does not establish absence from the model
  • 3 species Not in the project's own species list Use manual identification; this model's project checklist does not carry the species

Each of the 93 species sits in exactly one row. The frame counts and accuracy behind each status are in the species table on the model-health page, model_health_dashboard.html. Which species sits in which row is a column of per_species_health.csv.

Why this order, and how far to trust it

The reasoning behind the order above. Open a line to check it.

Why the queue is in this order

Ordering by look finds species faster than working down a random list. The charts below score that on photos already named and show where the ordering stops separating photos. The head of every queue a botanist works is on screen.

What this is measured on. The 1,719 photos a botanist has already named, carrying 155 species between them. Not the queue. It shows the method works where the answer is known.

Half those species take 129 photos in this order and 329 in a random one. Read it as evidence for the ordering, not as a promise about the queue: this run starts from nothing, and the queue starts from every photo already named.

How to read this: each point averages a slice of the queue. The line falls, so photos near the top really are less like the named ones than photos near the bottom. Most of that fall is in the first quarter of the queue, and past it the line keeps dropping, but slowly. The photos it separates there differ from each other far less than the ones at the head do.

Does this order find rare species faster than a random one? Measured on the 1,719 photos that already carry a name: 155 species, 107 of them rare, meaning 5 or fewer labelled frames. Over 8 random starts, this order found 162% more rare species over the run than a random order did, range 139% to 193%, p 0.004. 8 of 8 starts favoured this order over the random one.

How the runs were set up, and what they rest on

Each run starts from 200 photos picked at random and adds 20 a round for 20 rounds. It reached in 6 rounds what the random order reached in 20: 70% fewer labels for the same rare species. A separate check: 82% of these photos have a same-species nearest photo as the model sees them. When that photo must come from another flight, meaning another date or another site, it is 79%. This measurement stands on its own, with no prior expectation to check it against. The file of photo vectors was written 2026-09-17. No number here comes from a paper or from a different model. Each run ranked by how new the photo looks alone, with no queue first. The shipped order on this page sorts into the four queues first, so the gain describes the ranking alone, not this order.

Is it the species, or the batch? Of the 387 photos it puts first, 19% carry the newer file naming, against 11% across the queue: a later batch, not another camera. It was tested five times: site held fixed, export batch held fixed, the flight, meaning the date and the site, held fixed, then labelled-frames count per species held fixed. With every species lending the same number of labelled frames the link is +0.05, against +0.45 uncontrolled.

A photo can look new for the batch it came from rather than for what grows in it. A photo is measured against the nearest labelled frame, so a species with one has nothing of its own to be near. That is why the uncontrolled figure is largely a count of those frames.

  • On the labelled frames (1,719, 12 sites): the link holds once the site is held fixed. Rank correlation +0.47 before, +0.44 after, p under 0.001; the site explains 4% of how new a photo looks.
  • On the queued photos (3,873, 2 export batches): the link holds once the export batch is held fixed. Rank correlation +0.45 before, +0.45 after, p under 0.001; the export batch explains 0% of how new a photo looks.
  • On the queued photos (3,873, 17 sites): the link holds once the site is held fixed. Rank correlation +0.45 before, +0.41 after, p under 0.001; the site explains 7% of how new a photo looks.
  • On the queued photos (3,873, 52 flights): the link holds once the flight is held fixed. Rank correlation +0.45 before, +0.41 after, p under 0.001; the flight explains 11% of how new a photo looks.
  • On the labelled frames (1,564, 104 species): the link is mostly the labelled-frames count per species, and fades once it is held fixed. Rank correlation +0.45 before, +0.05 after, p under 0.001; the labelled-frames count per species explains 29% of how new a photo looks. 51 photos were left out because their species had too few labelled frames to lend an equal share.

On the labelled photos the species is known, and on the queue it is the one the model guessed. Tested with labelfirst 0.9.0 on this checkout, written 2026-09-17. The file of photo vectors was 1eab5d4206b4 for the queue and f64c62db927e for the labelled photos.

Which frames can wait, and why the line sits where it does

Read this to move the confidence line, or to check the wait rule.

Suggested rule: leave a frame for later when Pl@ntNet is at least 0.8 confident and its species already has 10 or more labelled frames. On the 323 frames held back for grading, that rule reaches 152 of them (47.1%), and the first guess is wrong on 3.3% of those.

What those 323 frames are: the labelled frames held back for grading. The rule was chosen on the other frames, so no frame here helped pick the rule, and every count below is out of those 323. They are not the model-health page's 300 frames fixed in advance; the two overlap and neither contains the other.

The same rule on flights held back whole: it reaches 206 of the 323 frames there, and the first guess is wrong on 0.5% of those. Those frames hold different species in different shares, so how many the rule reaches differs for that reason alone.

Why grade it on those flights too

The frames above were held back one by one, so each shares a flight with a frame the rule was picked on. Frames from one flight overlap, so one can be a near-copy of another. No frame above shares a flight with the ones held back whole.

What a wait does, and how long it lasts

Nothing here is a label. A frame that can wait keeps whatever label it has, or none; the rule only pushes it down the queue. The decision also expires with the model. Pl@ntNet ships a new one every few months, and re-running this page after that can bring any frame back to the top.

Where the species half of the rule is counted

36 species reach 10 labelled frames inside the frames a rule may learn from, which is the second half of the rule. Counting every label gives a larger number, so this will not match the “too few labels to judge” count above.

Can we trust the confidence? In bulk yes, on rare species no

Over all frames at once the score is trustworthy: when the model is sure it is almost always right. That is what makes ordering the queue possible at all. On rarely-labelled species it is not, so ordering on confidence alone would push exactly the species you care about to the bottom:

Raising the confidence line does not repair this. Requiring the species to have been measured first does, which is why the suggested rule has two conditions.
